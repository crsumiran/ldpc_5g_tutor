# 04 · The Barrel Rotator

> **Goal:** Build the simplest real rotator, see why its control logic is free, and learn the physical-design issue that its textbook diagram hides.
>
> * **RTL:** `rtl/rot_barrel.v`
> * **Python Model:** `model/rotators.py::rotl_barrel`

## 1. The Idea: Binary Decomposition of the Shift

A rotator has to move every lane by the same amount s, where s can be anything from 0 to N − 1. Building a separate path for every possible s would be expensive. The barrel rotator avoids that by noticing that any shift s < N is a sum of powers of two, given directly by its bits:

```
s = s[0]·1 + s[1]·2 + s[2]·4 + …
```

Rotating by s can therefore be done as a sequence of fixed, conditional rotations:

> rotate by 1 if `s[0]`, then by 2 if `s[1]`, then by 4 if `s[2]`, …

Each step is one column of N 2:1 muxes that either passes the data straight through or rotates it by a fixed power of two. **The select of stage k is just `shift[k]`.** There is no decoding, no lookup, and no arithmetic. That is why the Song paper says that for the barrel shifter "the SV is the natural control signal".

## 2. Walkthrough: N = 8, s = 5 (Binary 101)

With s = 5 = 101₂, stages 0 and 2 rotate and stage 1 passes the data through:

```
input                          A B C D E F G H
stage 0  rotate by 1? s[0]=1 → B C D E F G H A
stage 1  rotate by 2? s[1]=0 → B C D E F G H A   (pass through)
stage 2  rotate by 4? s[2]=1 → F G H A B C D E
expected rotl(x, 5)          → F G H A B C D E   ✓
```

This trace is printed by the model, and the RTL testbench checks every shift.

### How to Read the Barrel Rotator Diagram

* **Every mux in a stage gets the same select, a single shift bit:** Stage 0 and stage 2 have `shift[k] = 1`, so all 16 of their muxes take the lower input, the "long" wire from 2^k lanes further down. Stage 1 has `shift[1] = 0`, so its 8 muxes pass straight through. There is no other control logic.
* **Each mux's lower input is a fixed wire:** In stage k, lane i's mux always connects to lane `(i + 2^k) mod 8`. The wires differ only in how far they reach: 1 lane in stage 0, 2 in stage 1, 4 in stage 2.
* **The dashed wires are the wrap-around:** These are the lanes where `i + 2^k ≥ N`, so the source is back at the top of the bus. In stage 2, every wire spans half the datapath: lanes 0–3 read from lanes 4–7, and lanes 4–7 wrap back to read from lanes 0–3. Picture that at N = 384 with 8-bit lanes and you have the wiring problem of Section 5.
* **The wrap point is fixed at lane N:** It is built into the dashed wires. This is why a barrel rotator cannot rotate a shorter vector (Section 6 and Chapter 05).

## 3. The RTL

The whole rotator in `rtl/rot_barrel.v` is two nested generate loops, one over stages and one over lanes:

```verilog
for (k = 0; k < SW; k = k + 1) begin : g_stage
    for (i = 0; i < N; i = i + 1) begin : g_lane
        localparam SRC = (i + (1 << k)) % N;          // fixed wire, computed at elaboration
        assign stage[k+1][i*W +: W] = shift[k] ? stage[k][SRC*W +: W]
                                               : stage[k][i*W +: W];
    end
end
```

Three points to notice:

* **`SRC` is a constant:** Each mux has one input that is a straight wire and one that is a fixed long wire. All the "intelligence" is in the wiring.
* **N does not have to be a power of two:** Stage k rotates by `2^k mod N`, and rotations compose, so any shift `< N` works. The paper's Fig. 2 is a 7 × 7 barrel shifter, and the testbench checks both N = 7 and N = 384.
* **The stage order is free:** Rotations commute, so you can put the 2^8 stage first or last. That matters for layout (Section 5).

## 4. Cost and Timing

| | Value | N = 384, W = 8 |
|---|---|---|
| Stages (mux levels on critical path) | ⌈log₂ N⌉ | 9 |
| 2:1 muxes per bit | N·⌈log₂ N⌉ | 3,456 |
| Total 2:1 muxes | × W | 27,648 |
| Control logic | none (shift bits drive selects directly) | 0 |
| Fan-out of each shift bit | N × W mux selects | 3,072 → needs a buffer tree |

Nine mux levels plus wire delay fit in one cycle at moderate clock rates. For high clock rates, put a pipeline register after stage 4 or so. **Because the stages commute, you can choose where to cut without changing the function.** The shift bits for the later stages must then be delayed to match.

## 5. What the Textbook Picture Hides: Wires

The gate count in Section 4 makes the barrel rotator look cheap, but it says nothing about wire length. In stage k, every lane has a wire to the lane 2^k away. In the last stage of a 384-lane rotator, that is 256 lanes away. With 8-bit lanes laid out side by side, lanes 0–127 each have a wire crossing two-thirds of the datapath (256 lanes), lanes 128–383 wrap back 128 lanes, and in some places hundreds of these wires overlap. **At large N, barrel shifters are usually wire-limited, not gate-limited.**

This has three practical consequences:

* **Floorplan the long stages deliberately:** Place the long-span stages where routing resources are richest, or fold the layout (interleave lanes `0, N−1, 1, N−2, …`) to shorten the worst wires.
* **Trust post-route timing, not post-synthesis timing:** After synthesis, compare post-route timing with post-synthesis timing. Expect them to disagree for rotators.
* **Consider recursive networks:** This is another reason Banyan-style networks are attractive. Their recursive structure maps to a recursive floorplan.

## 6. What the Barrel Rotator **Cannot** Do

The barrel rotator's fixed wiring is what makes its control free, and it is also what limits it:

1. **Rotate a vector shorter than N:** With N = 8 and a 5-element vector `[A B C D E . . .]`, rotation by 2 should give `[C D E A B]`, but the barrel rotator gives `[C D E . . . A B]`. Its wrap-around point is fixed at lane N. Chapter 05 fixes this.
2. **Process several small vectors independently:** Every stage moves data across the whole bus, so you cannot split it into two half-size rotators with different shifts. Chapter 06 fixes this with a Banyan.
